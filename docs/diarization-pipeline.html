Who Said What

Two models run over the same audio and never speak to each other. One returns words with no speaker; the other returns speakers with no words. Everything below is the machinery that puts them back together — and the one place it reliably fails.

The shape of it

Diarisation and recognition are deliberately independent. They meet exactly once, in SpeakerAlignment.blocks, and everything after that is cleanup.

Five stages

Numbered because the order is load-bearing, not for decoration. Each entry names the file and line you'd open to change it.

01

Split the audio by voice

diarise SpeakerDiarizer.kt:169 → :176

fun diarize(samples: FloatArray): List<DiarizedSegment>

A thin wrapper around one native call. Segmentation, an embedding per window, and clustering all happen inside active.process(samples) — nothing in Kotlin makes the "same voice" judgement. The wrapper converts sherpa's seconds into sample indices, drops zero-length segments, sorts by start, and logs per-cluster totals.

A cluster is an arbitrary integer, not a person. Clustering fails by over-segmenting: one speaker routinely becomes several clusters.

minDurationOn = 0.2f — nothing shorter becomes a turn (SpeakerDiarizer.kt:38) windowShiftRatio = 0.25f — accuracy/time knob; 0.10 costs 2.5× the runtime for +0.5%

02

Fold fragments, then put names on clusters

diarise SpeakerRepository.kt:398 · :320

suspend fun mergeSmallClusters(samples, turns): List<DiarizedSegment> suspend fun labelClusters(samples, turns): Map<Int, String>

Fragments too short to be a speaker are folded into whichever cluster they sound like — before anything downstream inherits their ids. Then each surviving cluster has up to 30 s of its longest turns concatenated, embedded as one voiceprint, and matched against the enrolled set.

No match becomes Unknown Speaker N, numbered by first appearance — so "Speaker 2" is the second person heard, not cluster index 2.

MATCH_THRESHOLD = 0.6f MATCH_MARGIN = 0.05f LABEL_SAMPLE_SECONDS = 30

03

Get words and their times

transcribe DiarizeWorker.kt:168 · TimedWords.kt:4

data class TimedWord(text: String, startSeconds: Float, endSeconds: Float)

Runs on its own slicing, chosen for whatever decodes best — the recogniser is never cut at speaker boundaries. sherpa returns one timestamp per token, and a token is a BPE piece; TimedWords assembles them into whole words, because half a word has no answer to "who was speaking when this was said".

This stage owns the progress bar outright. Diarisation cannot report progress — processWithCallback aborts the process on this toolchain.

04

The join — one word at a time

meet SpeakerAlignment.kt:53

fun blocks(words, turns, sampleRate): List<SpeakerBlock>

For each word it computes an evidence sample — not the midpoint. TimedWords closes each word at the next word's start, so a pause makes a one-syllable word look seconds long and the midpoint drifts into silence or the next speaker.

evidence = start + min(duration / 2, 0.2s) MAX_WORD_EVIDENCE_OFFSET_SECONDS, :146

Consecutive words sharing a cluster become one SpeakerBlock. See the decision table below for the four cases.

05

Name, merge, and absorb fragments

cleanup SpeakerBlockNaming.kt:39 · :114

fun nameBlocks(blocks, names, unknownLabel): List<NamedBlock> fun smoothShortBlocks(blocks, unknownPrefix, minSamples): List<NamedBlock>

Adjacent blocks merge on the name, never the cluster id — one person is routinely several clusters all correctly given the same name, and cutting on the id split continuous speech at every hop between them.

Then anything under 2 seconds adopts a neighbour's name — but only when both sides already agree, so nothing is claimed that the surrounding audio does not already say. A fragment between two different named people is left alone. Runs to a fixed point, because absorbing one fragment can make its neighbours agree.

SHORT_BLOCK_SECONDS = 2 MAX_SMOOTHING_PASSES = 4

The name is then written down on the row, not re-resolved on read. A block records who the app believed was speaking at the time it decided — so enrolling someone next week cannot silently rewrite last week's transcript.

How one word picks a speaker

Four cases, in order. The third and fourth are where the design refuses to guess.

Turns covering the evidence sampleResultWhy
exactly one that cluster The ordinary case.
several, incl. current speaker keep current speaker pyannote reports overlapping speech. Continuity is better evidence than picking whichever segment started first.
several, excl. current speaker UNATTRIBUTED (−1) An overlap with no established speaker is honestly unknown. Choosing one would invent certainty the model did not provide.
none (a gap) inherit only if the turns either side agree Diarisation reports confident speech, not continuous coverage. This keeps a breath inside someone's paragraph without carrying them across a gap into another person's turn.

Why align afterwards at all? The earlier design cut the audio at every speaker change and transcribed each turn separately. That made diarisation quality control transcription quality — a boundary off by a second sliced a word in half and both sides lost it. Aligning after the fact means a misplaced boundary merely moves a word between speakers: visible, and correctable.

Where it breaks, in real data

The first 50 seconds of eleven_two_voice.wav — ground truth against what the pipeline actually produced on the tablet.

Everything ≥ 2 s is correct. Everything wrong is a backchannel.

Across all three benchmark files, not one turn of two seconds or longer went to the wrong person. Every error is a one-word "Mhm.", "Yeah.", "Okay." The 2-second floor that fixes fragmentation is the same rule that erases genuine short turns — and the embedding model has no margin to separate two same-gender voices anyway (0.73 cosine between speakers, against 0.91 within one speaker's own takes).

Three different things get called a "turn" in this codebase, and they do not correspond one to one: a DiarizedSegment (machine output), a truth turn in .truth.json (a real conversational turn), and a DialogTurn (display grouping). One run gave 22 blocks against 36 truth turns.
