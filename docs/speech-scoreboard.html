The pipeline

How a recording becomes a labelled transcript

Audio is captured at 16 kHz in mono. Five stages then run over it, and the results below exercise all of them at once.

  1. A segmentation model cuts the audio into turns, meaning stretches where one person is talking. It treats silence as a cue for where a turn ends.
  2. An embedding model converts each turn into a voiceprint: a vector that describes the voice itself rather than the words.
  3. Those voiceprints are clustered, so turns spoken by the same person group together. When nobody has said how many people are present, the clustering cuts at a 0.5 similarity threshold. Small fragments are folded into larger clusters afterwards.
  4. Each cluster is compared against the voiceprints of people who have enrolled, and takes the closest name. Clusters with no close match are left unnamed.
  5. A speech recogniser transcribes the audio, and its word timings are matched against the turn ranges, so every word ends up underneath a speaker.

Enrolment, in step 4, means recording a few seconds of someone speaking so the app has a voiceprint to compare against later. How it was done here bounds what the results mean, and the table caption returns to it.

One option changes what stage 1 receives. When it is switched on, the silent parts of the recording are cut out first and only the speech is passed to attribution, which saves memory and time on a recording with long pauses in it. The recogniser in step 5 still receives the whole recording, because a word the detector missed should still be transcribed. The "silence cut" column in the table reports how much of each recording was removed this way.

What was used

Each is downloaded once and then runs locally through sherpa-onnx. Sizes are the files as they sit on disk after download, 683 MB together.

Parakeet is used because attribution needs per-word timings for step 5, and it is one of only two recognisers available here that report them.

Everything runs on the CPU, now through XNNPACK

There is no GPU or NPU path: the models are dynamically int8-quantised, which is a CPU strength rather than a GPU one. What changed since these runs is that the CPU work no longer takes the generic path. XNNPACK — ONNX Runtime's optimised ARM int8 kernels — is compiled into the prebuilt library alongside NNAPI and QNN, and is now the default execution provider: measurably faster on the int8 recogniser, and about 3% on the diarisation embeddings. NNAPI and QNN stay unused — NNAPI is deprecated and does not cover dynamic int8, and QNN needs models pre-compiled to Qualcomm context binaries. An earlier version of this note read the library as CPU-only; running provider=xnnpack on both tablets showed otherwise.

How to read the table

Voice sources and the five measures

The test audio was synthesised by ElevenLabs, whose voices are close enough to real speech that telling two of them apart is a fair test. Three appear here: Sarah, a woman, and Daniel and George, both men. Each turn of the conversation was synthesised separately and the pieces joined together, so the correct answer is known to the exact audio sample: who spoke, from when, to when.

The pairing matters more than the individual voices. A man and a woman is the easy case. Two men is the hard one, and it is hard for a measurable reason: the two male voiceprints sit at 0.734 cosine similarity, against 0.91 between two separate recordings of the same person. That gap is the ceiling every later stage inherits, because once a cluster contains both voices only one name can be attached to it and no later stage recovers the other.

Accuracy is reported five ways. The three speaker measures disagree with each other, and the size of the gap is itself informative: word and frame accuracy weight a speaker by how much they said, while turn accuracy counts every turn once however short it was.

Coverage
How much of the expected text the transcript actually reached. Read this before the error rate: below 90% the transcript stopped early, and its error rate means nothing.
WER
Word error rate. The share of words the recogniser got wrong, with numerals spelled out so that "14001" and "fourteen thousand and one" count as agreeing. Lower is better.
Speaker accuracy, by word
Of the words both transcripts agree were said, the share placed under the right speaker. This is the figure the app itself displays.
Frame accuracy
The share of 10 millisecond slices of speech attributed to the right person. It weights a speaker by how long they talked.
Turn accuracy
The share of conversational turns whose majority speaker is right. Every turn counts once, however short, which is why this is the harshest of the three and the closest to how a reader experiences the transcript.

The two devices produce the same numbers, not similar ones

English and German were run on both tablets and matched down to the frame count: 27,009 of 27,910 speech frames on both, 27 blocks on both, the same 29 turns out of 36. German matched at 17,016 of 17,846 frames and produced identical clustering, four clusters folding to two. Attribution is deterministic given the same audio and the same models, so these figures are not a quirk of one tablet. The two English rows reading 98.6% and 99.0% are two different enrolments rather than two different devices, and both tablets reproduced both values exactly.

Added since these runs · 27 August 2026

A faster recogniser, a faster embedder, one ceiling that holds

The recogniser in the table is Parakeet, 639 MB, chosen for its per-word timings. A smaller sibling has since joined it: NeMo FastConformer, 105 MB, covering English, German, Spanish and French and reporting the same timings. On the German file above it scored 2.47% word error against Parakeet's 2.5% — level — and it transcribes about three times faster, a twenty-minute recording's transcription falling from 114.7 to 38.9 seconds. On dense English it costs roughly 1.3 points of word error, so both stay on the shelf: Parakeet for accuracy, FastConformer for speed and a fifth of the memory.

Combined with the pipeline running its two halves at once, folding and naming sharing a single embedding pass, two chunks diarised in parallel, and the XNNPACK default above, that swap took the whole speaker job on a twenty-minute recording from about 115 seconds to 55 — with every accuracy figure unchanged, because attribution is deterministic and the same audio and models return the same numbers.

A faster voiceprint model was added too — 3D-Speaker's CAM++, 192 dimensions against ERes2Net's 512, about 2.8× quicker — and it sharpens the ceiling this page describes rather than lifting it. On the two-men pairing it separates less well: enrolled from their own voices, the German men scored 0.932 and 0.929 against one cluster, a 0.003 gap the naming guard refuses to guess on, so only one was named and speaker accuracy sat at 59% where ERes2Net — the model behind the table's 98.2% on the same file — keeps them apart. CAM++ trades that separation for speed, so both remain a choice. A clean man-and-woman pair separates under either: enrolled the same way, both named with a 0.5 margin and 99.8% of words on the right speaker. Separable voiceprints are the whole game, and enrolment — a naming step — cannot separate what clustering could not.
